Sciatica: Causes, Symptoms & Physiotherapy Treatment in Wayanad

Written byDr. Sandra Thomas (PT)
14 August 2026
9 min read
Panamaram, Wayanad

Sciatica describes pain or other nerve-related symptoms that travel along the path of the sciatic nerve, usually from the lower back or buttock into the back of the thigh and leg. For many people, symptoms improve with appropriate conservative care, but the right treatment depends on the cause, severity and neurological findings.

If you are experiencing back pain with leg pain, tingling, numbness or weakness, a proper clinical assessment is important before choosing exercises. At Physio Dynamics in Panamaram, Wayanad, physiotherapy assessment focuses on your symptoms, movement, strength, sensation and functional limitations so that rehabilitation can be tailored to you.

What Is Sciatica?

The term sciatica is commonly used for pain or neurological symptoms related to irritation or compression of a lumbar nerve root. A disc problem is one possible cause, but it is not the only one. Other spinal or surrounding tissue problems can produce similar symptoms.

Sciatica should also be distinguished from ordinary low back pain that simply refers discomfort into the buttock or leg. Because different conditions can look similar, a physiotherapist or doctor should assess the pattern of symptoms rather than assuming that every episode of leg pain is sciatica.

Common Causes of Sciatica

Sciatica can have several possible causes. Common examples include:

  • Herniated or bulging lumbar disc: Disc material can irritate a nearby nerve root and cause radiating leg symptoms.
  • Age-related spinal changes: Changes around the joints and discs may reduce the available space around a nerve root.
  • Lumbar spinal stenosis: Narrowing around spinal nerves can sometimes contribute to leg pain, numbness or heaviness.
  • Spondylolisthesis and other spinal conditions: Changes in spinal alignment can affect the structures surrounding nerve roots.
  • Other sources of leg pain: Musculoskeletal conditions can sometimes mimic sciatica, which is why clinical assessment matters.

Sciatica Symptoms to Watch For

Sciatica does not feel exactly the same for everyone. Symptoms may occur in the lower back, buttock, thigh, calf or foot and may include:

  • Pain travelling from the lower back or buttock into one leg.
  • Burning, electric, shooting or aching pain.
  • Tingling or pins-and-needles.
  • Numbness in part of the leg or foot.
  • Muscle weakness or difficulty with certain movements.
  • Symptoms that change with sitting, standing, walking or particular spinal movements.

Understanding Your Recovery

  1. Assessment: Your symptoms, neurological status, movement and functional limitations are reviewed.
  2. Early management: Education, comfortable movement and appropriate activity modification help you stay as active as possible.
  3. Progressive rehabilitation: Exercises are gradually progressed according to pain, strength, mobility and function.
  4. Return to normal activity: Strength, endurance, lifting, walking and work or sport demands are rebuilt progressively.

How Physiotherapy Assesses Sciatica

A physiotherapy assessment is more than simply finding the most painful spot. Depending on your presentation, your physiotherapist may examine:

  • Location, intensity and behaviour of your back and leg symptoms.
  • Lower-limb strength and functional movement.
  • Sensation and relevant neurological findings.
  • Spinal and hip movement patterns.
  • Walking, sitting, lifting and other activities that aggravate or ease symptoms.
  • Warning signs that require medical referral or further investigation.

This assessment helps determine whether your symptoms are appropriate for physiotherapy management or whether you should first be reviewed by another healthcare professional.

Physiotherapy Treatment for Sciatica

Current clinical guidance emphasizes active, individualized management rather than prolonged bed rest. Physiotherapy may combine education, exercise and functional rehabilitation, with other hands-on approaches used when appropriate.

1. Education & Activity Advice

Understanding your symptoms can reduce fear and help you make sensible decisions about movement. In many cases, staying gently active and gradually returning to normal activities is preferable to prolonged inactivity.

2. Individualized Exercise Therapy

Exercises are selected according to your clinical presentation rather than using one routine for everyone. Depending on your assessment, a programme may include repeated movement exercises, trunk and hip strengthening, mobility work, walking progression and general conditioning.

3. Neural Mobility When Appropriate

Some people may benefit from carefully selected nerve-mobility exercises. These should be prescribed according to symptoms and response, because aggressive stretching of an irritable nerve can increase symptoms.

4. Manual Therapy as an Adjunct

Hands-on techniques may be used alongside an active exercise programme when they are clinically appropriate. The goal is to support movement and function rather than make passive treatment the only part of rehabilitation.

5. Functional Rehabilitation

As symptoms settle, rehabilitation should progress toward real-life activities such as walking, prolonged sitting, lifting, work tasks, household activities or sport. Your programme can be adjusted according to the demands you need to return to.

Gentle Home Exercises: What to Consider

There is no single “best exercise” for every person with sciatica. The exercise that is appropriate for you depends on the direction of symptoms, irritability, strength, mobility and neurological findings.

  • Walking: Short, comfortable walks can be used as a starting point if walking does not significantly worsen symptoms.
  • Gentle movement: Comfortable spinal or hip movements may help maintain mobility.
  • Trunk and hip strengthening: Progressive strengthening can be introduced as tolerated.
  • Activity pacing: Break prolonged sitting or other aggravating activities into manageable periods.

Important: Do not force an exercise because it is advertised online as a universal sciatica cure. If an exercise causes a clear increase in leg pain, numbness or weakness, stop and seek professional advice.

Sciatica Red Flags: When to Seek Urgent Care

Most episodes of back pain and sciatica are not caused by a medical emergency, but certain symptoms require urgent assessment. Seek urgent medical attention if you develop:

  • New difficulty controlling your bladder or bowel.
  • Numbness around the saddle, genital or inner-thigh area.
  • Severe or rapidly worsening weakness in one or both legs.
  • Major changes in walking associated with worsening neurological symptoms.
  • Severe symptoms accompanied by other signs suggesting serious spinal or systemic disease.

These symptoms can indicate serious nerve involvement and should not be managed by continuing home exercises without medical assessment.

Do You Need an MRI for Sciatica?

An MRI is not automatically necessary for every person with sciatica. Imaging is generally considered when there are concerning features, progressive neurological symptoms, persistent symptoms despite appropriate management, or when imaging is likely to change treatment decisions.

A scan can show structural changes that are also present in people without pain, so MRI findings should be interpreted together with the clinical examination and symptoms.

When Should You See a Physiotherapist?

Consider a physiotherapy assessment if leg pain is affecting walking, sitting, sleep, work, exercise or daily activities, or if symptoms are not improving as expected. Early assessment can help identify appropriate activity levels, provide a structured exercise plan and determine whether medical referral is needed.
